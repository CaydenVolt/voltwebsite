Why 20% of your signed solar deals cancel before install, and the three fixes that actually work

## Body

Most solar owners track the close rate obsessively and barely look at the cancellation rate. Then they wonder why revenue does not match what the pipeline says it should.

Residential solar cancellation rates sit between 15 and 25 percent for most installers in 2026, and above 30 percent at companies that lost control of the process after the federal credit changed. The signed contract is not the sale. The deal closes when the system is energised, and roughly one in five contracts never gets there. The three fixes below are what separates the installers whose signed pipeline converts to revenue from the ones who sell twice to collect once.

This is the operator maths we take through with Volt clients when they tell us their close rate is up but their install volume is flat. The gap is almost always in the fourteen days after signature.

## Where is the 20 percent actually going?

Cancellation is not one problem, it is five, and they come in roughly these proportions from the installers we work with. Yours will differ, but the ratios are usually in this order.

**Finance declines (around 35 to 45 percent of cancellations).** The lender comes back with a decline, a higher rate than quoted, or terms the homeowner will not sign for. This got worse in 2026. Lenders tightened criteria after the credit expired for cash buyers, dealer fees moved to the installer side of the paperwork, and the share of applications that come back at a worse rate than the sales rep quoted went up materially. Most installers only find out at the point the customer calls to back out.

**Site survey or HOA kills (around 20 to 25 percent).** The site survey finds something the sales rep did not. A structural issue, panel placement constraints, service upgrade required, HOA covenant restricting roof-mount installs, interconnection capacity limits. In some states, interconnection delays alone now kill 10 percent of deals because the queue stretches beyond the homeowner's patience.

**Competitor undercutting during the gap (around 10 to 15 percent).** The homeowner was shopping the whole time, not just before signing. Somebody else called them during the two weeks after your contract, quoted lower, and your rep was not in the loop that the comparison was still happening.

**Buyer's remorse with no identifiable cause (around 10 to 15 percent).** They slept on it, read something that spooked them, got a hard question from a family member, or simply went cold. These are the hardest to prevent because there is no specific issue to solve. What you can control is the touchpoint cadence that keeps the relationship warm.

**Life change (around 5 to 10 percent).** Job loss, moving, divorce, medical. Genuinely out of your hands, and the only honest response is a clean cancellation process that leaves the door open for the next time.

The first three are where the leverage is. They account for three quarters of your cancellations and all three are addressable with process.

## Fix one: qualify finance before the contract, not after

Most cancellations from the finance bucket happen because the sales rep quoted a payment based on best-case terms and the actual approval came back worse. The homeowner feels misled, which they are, and uses that as the reason to cancel.

The fix is a soft credit pull during the sales conversation and a bill verification before the contract is presented. Every mainstream solar lender now supports soft pulls that do not affect the homeowner's credit score. The data a soft pull returns is not the final approval, but it is enough to see the FICO band and the debt-to-income ratio that determines which tier the application will land in.

**Operator maths on this one:** a sales rep who runs soft pulls on every serious lead adds about seven minutes to the call. Across one hundred leads, that is twelve hours. The same rep stops quoting payments that come back at a 300-basis-point markup. Finance-driven cancellations drop by half to two-thirds inside three months. For a company doing one hundred installs a year with a 20 percent cancellation rate, roughly eight to twelve more installs survive, which is $120,000 to $200,000 in revenue the rep's twelve hours of credit-pull calls earned back.

Running a soft pull on every lead feels aggressive. Running it on every serious lead who has shown bill, roof and signed intent to buy is just the lender-side equivalent of the site survey.

## Fix two: a documented five-day install-readiness protocol

The site survey is where ten to fifteen percent of your cancellations are decided, and most installers run it inconsistently. The fix is a written protocol that every signed contract runs through within five days of signature, in the same order, with the same checks.

Minimum items on the list:

- Site survey scheduled within 48 hours of signature
- Electrical panel photographed and sent to the project engineer for load calculation before survey
- HOA or covenant check completed and documented
- Utility interconnection application submitted within 72 hours
- Permit application submitted within 72 hours
- Customer called on day 3 after signature by the project manager, not the salesperson

The reason every one of these has a specific timeframe attached is that the gap between signature and the first install-side action is where the deal goes cold. A customer who signs on Monday and hears nothing until the following Tuesday has had seven days of competitor outreach, family pressure and buyer's doubt with no counter-messaging from you.

This is also the fix that separates the ten percent of installers who still convert well in 2026 from the ones whose close-to-install ratio dropped after the credit changed. A homeowner who signs a solar contract in 2026 is less committed than one who signed in 2024. They are weighing a bigger monthly payment against a less certain long-term saving. The install-readiness protocol is what keeps that decision from drifting.

## Fix three: scheduled touchpoints at day 1, day 7, and day 14

The cancellations attributed to buyer's remorse are not really remorse. They are drift. The customer had no reason to say no in the week they signed and no reason to say yes in the week they cancelled. The intervening fourteen days contained silence from you, and that silence is what the competitor filled.

Three touchpoints prevent most of this.

**Day 1: welcome call, not from the rep.** Twenty minutes with the project manager who will actually run the install. The call is to introduce them, confirm the install-readiness protocol, and answer anything the rep did not. Having a second named person involved makes cancellation harder because the customer now has two relationships to break, not one.

**Day 7: progress update, written.** A short email or text with the specific status of the survey, permit, utility application and finance. Not a template with placeholders. The actual status, with dates. If something is delayed, the message says so and names the next action. Status updates even when there is no progress are counter-intuitively what matters most, because silence reads as problems.

**Day 14: scheduled 15-minute call, calendar invite sent.** This is the single highest-leverage touchpoint. Fourteen days after signature is roughly when buyer's remorse peaks and when competitors' follow-ups land. A customer with a scheduled call on your calendar is a customer who has committed to staying in the process for at least two more weeks.

The whole sequence takes under an hour of project manager time per contract, costs effectively nothing, and in our client data cuts cancellations by 40 to 60 percent against the "wait for the install to happen" default.

## How to measure whether any of this worked

Three numbers, tracked monthly, from contract date forward.

**Cancellation rate by cause.** The five buckets above. Most CRMs let you tag the reason on the cancellation record. Without the tagging you cannot tell which fix moved what.

**Time from signature to first site action.** The gap is where deals die. A median of two days is healthy. A median of eight days is why your cancellations are at 25 percent.

**Install-to-contract ratio by cohort.** Signed contracts from one calendar month, tracked as a cohort through to install or cancellation. Install-to-contract ratio rising by 5 percentage points inside a quarter is the leading indicator that the process changes are working, long before revenue catches up.

## The honest closing line

The sales team cannot fix this on their own. Finance qualification, site surveys, utility applications and project management touchpoints are not things a rep can push through. This is operations and the sales team working the same sheet, and the companies whose cancellation rate stays low in 2026 are the ones who treat the two weeks after signature as seriously as the two weeks before it.

A signed contract is a lead on the install. Not a sale.
