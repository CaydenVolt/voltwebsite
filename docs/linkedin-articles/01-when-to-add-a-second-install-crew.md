## Body

Most solar owners add the second install crew either six months too early or six months too late. The ones who get it right stopped looking at the backlog and started looking at three specific numbers.

The right time to add a second install crew is when your current crew is at 85 to 95 percent utilisation for three consecutive months, your pipeline shows four to six weeks of booked installs ahead, and your cash reserves cover ninety days of the new crew's payroll before they contribute margin. Jump early and you burn runway on dead weight. Wait too long and your close rate drops because you can no longer promise an install date homeowners will accept.

This is the framework we use with Volt clients when the second-crew question comes up. It is operator maths, not a growth-at-all-costs playbook.

## What signals mean you actually need a second crew?

Four signals, in order of how reliable they are.

**Utilisation above 85 percent for three straight months.** Utilisation here means billable install days against available install days, after weather and permit delays. A crew that is physically on roofs 85 percent of the available days is at a sustainable ceiling. One at 95 percent is already losing days to burnout, bad weather make-ups and warranty callbacks. Three months is the horizon that filters out a lucky quarter from a real pattern. One hot month is seasonality. Three is a trend.

**Install backlog consistently above six weeks.** Six weeks is roughly the point at which homeowners start calling the sales rep weekly to ask when the install is happening, and the point at which a competitor who can promise four weeks starts winning deals you had already sold. Measure this as scheduled install date minus contract signing date, averaged across the last 20 contracts. Not minus permit approval; minus signing.

**Deals lost to timing, not price.** Pull your last 30 lost deals and sort by stated reason. If more than 20 percent of them list timing or install date as the reason, you have a capacity problem that is now a sales problem. Price objections are a different fix. Timing losses are the first dollar in your bank account that the second crew would have earned.

**Sales team hesitating to book.** The clearest signal and the hardest to see. When reps start adding caveats to install dates on calls, like "probably mid-November, could be December", the pipeline is already compressed. Ask three reps whether they are telling homeowners about the current wait. If they are, you are late.

If three of these four signals are hitting, the second crew pays for itself inside six months.

## What does a second crew actually cost in the first ninety days?

Rough US ranges for a residential solar crew of foreman plus two installers:

- Foreman salary plus taxes plus benefits: $18,000 to $25,000 over the first ninety days
- Two installer salaries plus taxes plus benefits: $22,000 to $32,000
- Workers comp premium at the solar class: $4,000 to $8,000
- Install truck (used), racks and tools: $20,000 to $45,000 as a one-time cost
- General liability bump: $500 to $1,500
- Project manager time to onboard the new crew: $3,000 to $6,000 of opportunity cost
- Permit fees and uniforms: $500 to $1,500

Total ninety-day cash commitment: roughly $68,000 on the low end, $120,000 on the high end, depending on whether you buy new or used equipment and your state's workers comp rates. Workers comp alone is the line item owners most consistently underestimate. Solar install is a high-mod class in most states, which is why insurance comes to the number it does.

What this does not include: the capex-smoothing you get from leasing the truck, financing the equipment or using an existing warehouse. In practice most owners spread the equipment cost over 24 to 36 months, which reduces the first-ninety-days cash hit by $12,000 to $25,000.

## What is the right ramp sequence for a new crew?

New crews that go independent on day one are the ones you see on the news for a bad install. The ramp that works:

**Week 1 to 2: hire and onboard.** Paperwork, safety training, equipment setup, paired shadowing on the senior crew. Zero installs attempted by the new crew in week one. One or two in week two, with the senior foreman present.

**Week 3 to 4: paired installs.** The new foreman runs the install with the senior foreman watching. The senior foreman stays off the roof and only intervenes if something is being done unsafely or incorrectly. Three to five installs across these two weeks.

**Week 5 to 8: supervised solo.** The new crew runs installs on their own with the senior foreman doing a morning briefing and an afternoon walk-through. Six to ten installs across the four weeks. This is where you catch the systematic errors, like consistent attachment issues or wire management problems, before they become ten warranty calls.

**Week 9 onward: independent.** The new crew runs their own calendar. The senior foreman is still available by phone for anything unusual but does not need to be on site.

An owner who skips the paired weeks saves two weeks of foreman time and buys back six months of warranty callbacks. Not a good trade.

## How do you know you are ready financially?

The ninety-day runway rule. Count the second crew's fully loaded payroll, taxes, benefits, insurance adjustment and vehicle finance for ninety days. Divide by current operating cash. If the answer is more than 30 percent of current cash, you are not ready. The crew will contribute margin in month three or four, but you need to be able to float them for the first ninety days without starving the rest of the business.

The second check is deposit timing. If you take a 10 percent deposit at signing and the rest at permit approval, your cash cycle on each install is six to twelve weeks. Doubling your install volume doubles that outstanding receivable. A business that has been running with $50,000 of installs-in-progress receivable at any given time becomes a business running with $100,000. Make sure the line of credit covers it.

The third check is finance company payout timing. If a large share of your revenue comes through a dealer-financed product, check the actual time from PTO to funding. Thirty days is the promise. Forty-five is more common. Ninety is not unheard of if the paperwork hits a snag. Doubling install volume doubles the amount of cash sitting with the finance company.

If all three checks pass, hire. If any fail, fix that first.

## What are the common mistakes owners make here?

**Hiring based on current demand only.** Current demand is the easy part. If your sales team is on fire this month, the second crew looks obvious. The question is whether that demand holds for the ninety days the crew needs to break even. Honest answer: look at your pipeline conversion rate for the last twelve weeks, not the last four.

**Not budgeting for the truck and equipment.** The payroll number is easy to see. The $35,000 for a used truck plus racks plus tools is the one that gets forgotten and then gets financed on the owner's personal credit card at 24 percent. Budget it, finance it properly, or defer the hire.

**Skipping the paired install period.** Covered above. The two-week delay feels expensive. The two years of warranty callbacks from an undertrained crew is more expensive.

**Hiring the foreman last.** The usual order is to hire two installers first and then promote one. This almost never works. A good installer is not automatically a good foreman. Hire the foreman first, then the installers to match the foreman's style.

**Treating the crew as fungible.** New crews drift toward the easiest work, like small residential and easy roofs. The senior crew gets the hard jobs by default. Six months in you have a two-crew operation where one crew produces 60 percent of the revenue and the other 40 percent, and you cannot tell the owner why. Assign jobs by rotation, not by preference.

## When should you NOT add a crew?

Three situations where the second crew makes things worse.

**Pipeline inflated by stale leads.** If your 4-to-6-week backlog is partly made up of leads from three months ago that nobody has called in two weeks, your real backlog is shorter than it looks. Clean the pipeline before you commit to doubling capacity.

**Lead quality dropping.** If close rates have fallen for two consecutive quarters, your sales system has a problem that the second install crew will not fix. More capacity on a weaker funnel just loses more deals faster.

**Operational issues in the current crew.** If the current crew's install quality, warranty callback rate or safety incidents are trending the wrong way, hire a trainer or a project manager before you hire a second crew. Doubling an operation with a quality problem doubles the quality problem.

Resolve these first. The second crew will still be there in a quarter when the underlying issue is fixed, and it will contribute margin faster when it does come on.

## What this looks like for a typical US residential solar company

A company doing 15 installs a month with one crew at 90 percent utilisation, pipeline sitting at seven weeks, and $280,000 in operating cash. Payroll plus benefits plus insurance for a second crew is roughly $14,000 per month fully loaded, with $35,000 of one-time equipment. Second crew runs 10 to 12 installs a month by month three. Revenue added per month at $22,000 average contract and 30 percent gross margin: $66,000 to $79,000 of additional gross margin. Second crew pays back the ninety-day investment in roughly four to five months and compounds from there.

A company at 10 installs a month with 65 percent utilisation, pipeline at four weeks, and $110,000 of operating cash should not be hiring a second crew. The maths does not work on either axis.

## FAQ

**Can one owner-operator manage two install crews?**
Yes, for the first six months, if the owner is willing to run project management for both crews personally. After six months most owners find that they are either neglecting sales or neglecting install quality. The honest answer is that the second crew usually forces the hire of a project manager within six to nine months, which is a line item worth budgeting from day one.

**Should I lease or buy the second install truck?**
Lease if the business is less than three years old and you expect install volume to double again within eighteen months. Buy used if the business is established and you are confident in the demand. New trucks almost never make financial sense for a solar operator; the depreciation hit in year one exceeds the maintenance you avoid.

**What if I only need a crew seasonally?**
Consider subcontractors or 1099 install crews for the peak quarters rather than a permanent second crew. The breakeven usually sits around nine months of billable work per year; below that, subcontractors are cheaper and more flexible. Above that, a W2 crew is more profitable and more controllable.

**When should I hire a project manager instead of a second install crew?**
When the install crew's utilisation is below 80 percent but their day is being eaten by permits, inspections, materials coordination and homeowner communication. That is a project management problem, not a capacity problem, and a $65,000-a-year project manager will add more install days than a $140,000-a-year install crew will.

**How much install experience should new installers have before I hire them?**
A foreman needs at least three years on US residential systems and ideally one year as a lead. Installers can be hired with less, including from adjacent trades like electricians, roofers or HVAC, if you have a strong training programme. The error most owners make is hiring installers with "experience" that turns out to be two months at a company that was itself badly trained.
