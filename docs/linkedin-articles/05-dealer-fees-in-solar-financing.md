Dealer fees in solar financing: a plain-English installer breakdown

## Body

Dealer fees are the quietest 10 to 25 percent of your gross margin, and most solar owners cannot explain them clearly to a homeowner. The result is a conversation where the rep pivots away from how the financing works, the homeowner reads that as evasion, and the deal cools. Reps and owners who understand dealer fees cold tend to win more deals and run healthier businesses.

A dealer fee is the discount a solar installer accepts to let a third-party finance company originate a long-term loan for the homeowner at a fixed interest rate. The fee is paid by the installer to the finance company, deducted from the installer's project revenue when the system funds, and ranges from 0 percent on cash deals to 30 to 40 percent on the lowest-rate 25-year loans. The fee buys the homeowner the low rate; it costs the installer margin. Understanding exactly where the money moves and why is the single most useful piece of finance literacy a solar company can carry.

This piece lays out how dealer fees work mechanically, why they vary so much across loan products, how to explain them honestly to a homeowner, and what the trade-offs are for your business.

## How does a dealer fee actually work mechanically?

A homeowner buys a solar system priced at $30,000. They sign a 25-year loan at 6.99 percent interest with a finance company. On paper, the loan is for the full $30,000 and the homeowner's monthly payment is calculated against that balance at that rate.

What actually moves: the finance company funds you (the installer) somewhere between $21,000 and $27,000 for that same $30,000 contract. The difference, between $3,000 and $9,000, is the dealer fee. The homeowner still owes $30,000 to the finance company over 25 years, but the money that reaches your bank account is the net amount after fee.

The finance company uses the dealer fee to subsidise the interest rate. A 25-year loan at 6.99 percent is uneconomic for the lender at the actual cost of capital in today's rate environment. The dealer fee is how the economics are made to work: the installer effectively prepays part of the interest for the homeowner, in exchange for the finance company carrying the full note at a rate the homeowner can accept.

That is the mechanical shape. Everything else is variations on it.

## Why does the fee range from 0 percent to 40 percent?

Three variables set the fee size:

**Loan term.** Longer term equals higher fee. A 10-year loan at 7.99 percent might carry an 8 to 15 percent dealer fee. A 25-year loan at the same 7.99 percent might carry 20 to 30 percent. The longer the finance company's capital is tied up, the more the dealer fee has to compensate.

**Interest rate presented to the homeowner.** Lower rate equals higher fee. A 25-year loan at 3.99 percent might carry 35 to 42 percent dealer fee. The same 25-year loan at 9.99 percent might carry 10 to 18 percent. The dealer fee buys the rate down.

**Finance product tier.** Finance companies offer a stack of products. The most aggressive rates come with the highest fees. Mid-tier products balance the two. The lowest-fee products come with higher rates or shorter terms.

Current US ranges across the major installer finance providers:

- Cash sale: 0 percent fee (homeowner pays directly, no finance company involved)
- 10-year loan, 6.99 to 8.99 percent rate: 8 to 15 percent fee
- 15-year loan, 6.99 to 7.99 percent rate: 15 to 22 percent fee
- 20-year loan, 5.99 to 7.49 percent rate: 20 to 30 percent fee
- 25-year loan, 3.99 to 6.99 percent rate: 25 to 40 percent fee
- Lease / PPA structures: structurally different, roughly 10 to 25 percent effective margin reduction on the installer side depending on structure

Rates and fees shift with the broader interest rate environment. These are ranges that have held through 2026 but will drift as macro conditions change.

## How much does the dealer fee cost your business?

A simplified example: a company doing 25 installs per month at $25,000 average system price. Monthly gross revenue appears to be $625,000. If 70 percent of installs run on a 25-year loan at a 28 percent dealer fee, and 30 percent run cash:

- Loan installs: 17.5 installs × $25,000 × 28 percent = $122,500 per month in dealer fees
- Cash installs: 7.5 × $25,000 = $187,500 at 0 percent fee
- Net revenue after fees: $502,500 ($625,000 minus $122,500)
- Dealer fee cost per month: $122,500
- Dealer fee cost per year: $1,470,000

If that company were able to shift 10 percentage points of its mix from 25-year loans to 10-year loans (which carry lower fees), the per-month saving would be:

- 2.5 fewer installs on 28 percent fee: saves $17,500
- 2.5 more installs on 12 percent fee: adds $7,500
- Net monthly margin recovery: $10,000, or $120,000 per year

Dealer fees are also the single largest line item in most solar installers' cost of goods sold, exceeding equipment, exceeding labour, exceeding roof access and inspection. For many installers it is the second largest line item in the whole P&L, after only the loaded cost of systems.

## How should a sales rep explain dealer fees to a homeowner?

Honestly and simply. The scripts that work:

**When a homeowner asks about cash versus financing:**
"The system is priced at $30,000. If you pay cash, that is what you pay. If you finance over 25 years at 3.99 percent, the finance company advances us a smaller amount up front, which we accept because they are carrying the loan at a low rate for a long time. That cost is called a dealer fee. It is why financed systems sometimes have a slightly higher sticker price than cash: part of the finance company's cost is built into the system price."

**When a homeowner asks why the "cash price" is different from the "financed price":**
"Two reasons. First, if you pay cash, we avoid a dealer fee of about 25 percent that we would otherwise owe the finance company. Some of that saving comes back to you. Second, cash removes any interest cost over 25 years, which saves you tens of thousands over the life of the system. The system itself is the same; the finance structure is different."

**When a homeowner compares two financing offers:**
"The two rates are structured differently. The 3.99 percent loan has a larger dealer fee, which keeps your rate low over 25 years. The 7.99 percent loan has a smaller dealer fee but a higher rate. Over the full term, the lower rate usually saves more money, but the higher rate means a lower system price if we're not using it. Your choice depends on whether you plan to pay the loan off early or carry it to term."

The reps who get in trouble are the ones who treat dealer fees as a secret, or who pretend the "finance" price and the "cash" price are two different products rather than the same product under different capital structures. Homeowners can tell when a rep is being evasive, and the deal cools because they are not sure what they are actually buying.

## What are the trade-offs in choosing finance products?

Three main trade-offs on your side:

**Margin per deal versus total addressable market.** A product with a high dealer fee opens up the homeowners who can only make the maths work with a low monthly payment. A product with a low dealer fee preserves margin but excludes the price-sensitive homeowners. A finance stack with only high-fee products closes more deals at lower margin per deal. A stack with only low-fee products protects margin but closes fewer deals. The right mix usually has three to five products at different fee / rate / term combinations and lets the rep match the product to the homeowner.

**Fast funding versus cheap funding.** Finance companies pay out at different speeds. Some fund within 10 days of PTO; others take 60 to 90 days. The faster-funding products often carry a higher dealer fee as the installer effectively pays for the speed. For companies with cash flow strain, the faster-funding product is worth the extra fee. For capitalised companies, the cheaper product is better.

**Diversification versus volume concentration.** Running most of your volume through one finance company gives you volume discounts, better rep support and sometimes marketing dollars. It also leaves you exposed if that finance company tightens underwriting, changes dealer fee rates or exits the solar space. Industry history is full of installers who were caught when a dominant finance partner changed terms overnight. The healthier mix runs 60 to 70 percent through a primary finance partner and 30 to 40 percent through two or three secondary partners.

## What are the common mistakes installers make here?

**Not knowing the per-deal fee at pricing time.** The sales rep closes a deal at $30,000 on a 25-year loan without checking what the actual dealer fee will be. The deal funds and the finance company deducts 32 percent instead of the 25 percent the rep assumed. The deal that looked profitable is now break-even. Fix: the quote system should calculate the net revenue after dealer fee at the moment the loan product is selected, not after the system funds.

**Hiding the dealer fee from the rep.** Some owners don't tell their reps the actual dealer fee rates. The thinking is "if the rep knew, they would push cash too hard." The result is reps who close deals without understanding the margin on them. Train reps on the fee structure. Trust them with it.

**Pricing the system identically for cash and financed.** The installer loses 25 to 30 percent of the project revenue on a financed deal but is charging the homeowner the same price as cash. The margin on cash is 30 percent higher than on financed, which argues for a cash incentive to the homeowner (3 to 5 percent discount) that is still margin-positive compared to the financed version. Not every installer does this, but the ones that do pick up a disproportionate share of the cash buyers.

**Not reconciling funded amount against expected net.** The dealer fee statement from the finance company should match what the quote system calculated at sale. Many installers do not reconcile. Over a year of 300 installs, a 1 to 2 percent average discrepancy between expected and actual funded amounts can be $75,000 to $150,000 of lost margin that nobody is tracking.

**Over-indexing on one product tier.** The rep's favourite product becomes the rep's only product. The customer who needs a different structure gets pushed into the familiar one, which costs the sale or costs the margin. The CRM should log which product was offered and which was selected, and the sales manager should review the mix weekly.

## What this looks like inside a healthy solar P&L

A company doing $7.5 million of installed revenue per year. 30 percent cash mix, 70 percent financed mix across three loan tiers. Weighted average dealer fee: 20 percent (because cash drags the average down).

- Gross revenue: $7,500,000
- Dealer fees: $1,050,000 (14 percent of gross; 20 percent of the 70 percent that is financed)
- Net revenue after dealer fees: $6,450,000
- Cost of equipment: $2,250,000
- Cost of install labour: $900,000
- Overhead: $1,100,000
- Operating profit: $2,200,000, or 29 percent of gross, 34 percent of net

Shifting 15 percent of financed mix from highest-tier dealer-fee product to a mid-tier product:
- Dealer fee reduction: ~$140,000 per year
- Net margin improvement: $140,000 to operating profit, 6 percent increase in operating profit

This is the quiet lever most owners don't pull. Not because they can't, but because they haven't mapped the finance stack carefully enough to know where the lever is.

## FAQ

**Can I pass dealer fees directly to the homeowner?**
In most US states, no, not transparently. Finance company agreements typically prohibit disclosing the exact dealer fee to the homeowner, and consumer finance laws complicate it further. What you can and should do is price your systems to recover the fee implicitly, and offer a cash discount that reflects the saved fee.

**Do dealer fees go down with volume?**
Yes. Most finance companies have volume tiers that reduce the dealer fee by 1 to 4 percentage points at higher commitment levels. The threshold is typically $5 to $10 million of annual funded volume for the first tier reduction. If you are approaching that threshold, the finance company's rep is usually willing to negotiate.

**Are leases and PPAs structurally different from loans in terms of dealer fees?**
Yes. Leases and PPAs do not have a "dealer fee" in the same mechanical sense. The equivalent is the capital provider's required margin, which varies but typically costs the installer 10 to 20 percent of what a cash sale would have returned. Lease and PPA mechanics are their own topic.

**How do I pick which finance company to run most of my volume through?**
Four questions, in order: (1) What is the dealer fee at your current and projected volume? (2) What is the funding speed from PTO to deposit? (3) What is the credit approval rate on your average homeowner profile? (4) How reactive is the account rep when something goes wrong? The right answer across all four is the right primary partner.

**Should I be building a cash-only sales motion?**
Cash-only is a different business, and only a minority of US residential homeowners can self-fund a $30,000 purchase. More useful: build a strong cash-first conversation that captures the 15 to 25 percent of homeowners who can pay cash, then run financing for the rest. Those cash deals carry 25 to 35 percent more margin and should be weighted that way in your pipeline reporting.
