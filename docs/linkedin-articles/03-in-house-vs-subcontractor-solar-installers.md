```
In-house vs subcontractor solar installers: the real cost breakdown
```

---

## Body

The in-house vs subcontract decision has become the second most expensive mistake solar owners make, right behind pricing. The reason is that the comparison most owners run is wrong: they compare the per-install subcontract fee against the hourly cost of W2 crew labour. That comparison ignores half the real cost of each option and leads to a hire that collapses margin, or a subcontract relationship that collapses quality.

A W2 in-house install crew costs roughly 15 to 25 percent more per install than a subcontracted crew when fully loaded, but it buys you install scheduling control, consistent quality, and tighter warranty response. A subcontracted crew costs less per install but introduces scheduling variance, inconsistent quality across sites, and slower warranty response because the installer has their own book of work to prioritise. The right choice depends on your volume, your geography, your margin position, and how much of your revenue flows through warranty.

This piece lays out the actual numbers on both sides, where the hidden costs are, and the hybrid structures that work for US residential installers at different stages.

## What does an in-house install crew actually cost?

Fully loaded, a three-person residential solar crew in the US runs between $14,500 and $21,000 per month in all-in costs when it is utilised at 85 to 90 percent. That is the number owners should carry in their heads, not the hourly wage.

The breakdown for a crew of foreman plus two installers:

**Payroll:**
- Foreman: $65,000 to $85,000 annual base
- Installer 1: $42,000 to $55,000
- Installer 2: $38,000 to $50,000
- Overtime provision: $8,000 to $15,000 per year total

**Payroll taxes and benefits (burden):** 22 to 32 percent of base
- Employer FICA: 7.65 percent
- Workers comp (solar install class in most states): 8 to 15 percent of payroll
- Health insurance contribution: $400 to $700 per employee per month
- Retirement match: 2 to 4 percent of payroll
- Paid time off (loaded cost of 10 days PTO plus holidays): roughly 4 percent

**Equipment and transport:**
- Install truck plus racks: $25,000 to $45,000 if purchased used, or $650 to $900 per month leased
- Fuel: $600 to $1,200 per month
- Tools: $5,000 to $8,000 one-time, plus $150 to $300 per month replacement
- Uniforms, safety gear, PPE: $200 to $400 per month

**Overhead allocation:**
- Project manager time, scheduling, materials coordination: $2,500 to $4,500 per month per crew
- General liability bump: $100 to $300 per month per crew

Monthly fully loaded cost at 85 percent utilisation: $14,500 to $21,000. Per-install cost at 10 to 12 installs per month: $1,200 to $2,100. Per-watt cost at an average 8kW system: $0.15 to $0.26.

## What does a subcontract install cost?

Subcontract install pricing in the US has settled into fairly tight bands. Current ranges for a residential rooftop install, excluding materials:

**Standard shingle roof, single-storey, straightforward layout:**
- $0.50 to $0.70 per watt installed
- 8kW system: $4,000 to $5,600 install labour

**Standard shingle, two-storey, straightforward layout:**
- $0.60 to $0.85 per watt
- 8kW system: $4,800 to $6,800

**Complex roof (tile, metal seam, multiple roof faces, difficult access):**
- $0.75 to $1.20 per watt
- 8kW system: $6,000 to $9,600

**Ground mount:**
- Highly variable. $0.80 to $1.80 per watt depending on trenching, soil, racking type

Compared with in-house per-install cost of $1,200 to $2,100, the subcontract route runs roughly 2.5 to 3.5 times more expensive per install at the invoice level. That is the comparison that pushes most owners toward in-house too quickly.

What the invoice line does not include:

**Lower utilisation risk.** You only pay the subcontractor when they are installing. An in-house crew is paid whether or not there is an install that week. A seasonal business that runs at 55 percent utilisation some months and 95 percent in others may spend less overall on subcontractors than on W2.

**No workers comp exposure on their crew.** Workers comp in the solar install class is 8 to 15 percent of payroll in most states. On a $300,000 crew payroll that is $24,000 to $45,000 per year. Subcontractors carry their own.

**No equipment capex.** Subcontractors come with their own trucks, racks, tools. No $35,000 outlay for a used truck.

**No management overhead.** The project manager still schedules the install, but doesn't manage the crew directly. Save roughly $800 to $1,500 per crew per month of PM time.

**Variable, not fixed.** When sales slow down, your install cost goes to zero. In-house cost does not.

Add those four lines up and the real comparison narrows considerably. For a company doing 10 installs a month, subcontract typically wins on total cost. For a company doing 20 installs a month and growing, in-house typically wins. The breakeven usually sits around 12 to 15 installs per month per potential crew, depending on your state's workers comp class rate.

## When should you keep installs in-house?

Five conditions where in-house wins the economic comparison and the operational one:

**Volume is 15+ installs per month, steady for six months.** Volume is what drives in-house economics. Below 15 installs a month the crew sits idle too often to recover its fixed cost.

**Your geographic concentration is tight.** An in-house crew drives from your warehouse to the install site and back. If 80 percent of your installs are within 45 minutes of your base, the drive-time efficiency works. If installs are scattered across a two-hour radius, the crew burns three to five hours a week in windshield time and the economics collapse.

**You are selling on install speed.** If your sales positioning includes "install within three weeks of signing" and you need to defend that, you can only do it with a crew that answers to you. Subcontractors prioritise their own book first.

**You carry a long warranty response commitment.** If you are offering 10-year workmanship warranties and taking warranty seriously, in-house gives you a crew that is accountable and reachable the day the callback comes in. A subcontractor's warranty response window is often five to ten business days.

**Your margins depend on consistent install quality.** If a bad install costs you a Google review, a referral loss and a cancelled contract from the next homeowner down the street, consistent quality is worth paying 15 to 25 percent more per install for. Luxury-brand installers and premium-market players almost always run in-house.

## When should you subcontract?

Three conditions where subcontract is the better call:

**Volume below 15 installs per month, or variable by season.** Below 15 the W2 crew sits idle. Variable volume means you pay full freight even in the slow month, which eats margin.

**Geographic spread is wide.** If your sales territory is three counties wide and your closest install is 90 minutes away, a local subcontractor near the install site saves you four hours of crew drive time. That is 50 percent of a day.

**You are early-stage and still finding your sales rhythm.** Fixed cost is risk. In the first 18 to 36 months of a solar business, when the sales engine is still being tuned, every $200,000 of fixed cost locked in is a $200,000 bet on next year's volume. Subcontracting lets you grow and shrink with demand.

## How do warranty and callback liability change?

This is the piece most owners don't price in.

**With an in-house crew:** when a callback comes in, you dispatch the crew. The cost of the callback is one install day of crew labour, which is already a sunk cost in your fully loaded number. If a callback rate is 2 percent, the crew does roughly one callback every month or two. Marginal cost is close to zero on a monthly basis.

**With a subcontractor:** the warranty terms matter enormously. Two common models:

Model A, warranty included: subcontractor warrants their work for 12 to 24 months. You pay the per-install rate and callbacks within the warranty window are at their cost. After the warranty window, you pay market rate for the callback. Workmanship warranty rates in the US on this model: $150 to $400 per hour for warranty callbacks.

Model B, warranty excluded: subcontractor warrants only against gross defect. Any callback is at your cost, from day one. You carry the full warranty obligation. Rate per callback: $150 to $350 per hour of callback labour plus truck roll.

If your lifetime workmanship warranty rate is 8 to 12 percent of installs (industry median), model A costs you nothing on the first wave of callbacks and model B costs you real money. A 500-install-per-year company with 10 percent callback rate running Model B pays $75,000 to $140,000 per year in warranty labour. On an in-house crew, those same callbacks ride in the fully loaded cost that was already there.

This is the quietest tilt toward in-house. Owners who have operated for five years with a 10-year workmanship warranty almost always migrate toward in-house because the warranty cost compounds.

## What are the hybrid models that work?

Not every company fits cleanly into one or the other. Three hybrid structures we see working:

**Core in-house, overflow sub.** You run one in-house crew that handles 80 percent of installs. When volume spikes or geography stretches, you book a local sub for the overflow. In-house gets the premium installs and the warranty callbacks. Sub gets the straightforward volume. Works when: you have the volume to justify one crew but not two, and your market is seasonal.

**Metro in-house, outlying areas subbed.** You have an in-house crew for installs within a defined radius (usually 45 to 60 minutes from base) and sub out installs beyond that radius. Works when: your sales radius is wider than your efficient install radius.

**W2 foreman plus 1099 helpers.** You employ a W2 foreman who runs installs and brings in 1099 helpers for the day of install. This splits the economics: the foreman is the quality anchor, the helpers are flexible capacity. Works when: your state's independent contractor rules permit 1099 helpers (check carefully: California, Washington, New York and a growing list of states have moved against this). The 1099 helpers must genuinely be independent, usually meaning they work for multiple contractors and bring some of their own tools.

Each hybrid balances a specific tension. Owners who try all three in sequence are usually just avoiding a decision; owners who match the structure to the actual constraint usually settle on one and stay there.

## What are the mistakes to avoid?

**Comparing per-install subcontract fee against in-house hourly wage.** Already covered. The $4,800 subcontract fee versus the $85,000 foreman salary is not a comparison; it is a confusion. Use fully loaded monthly cost at utilisation, divided by installs.

**Signing a subcontract agreement without clear warranty terms.** The single most expensive clause omission in solar subcontract agreements. Make sure the warranty obligation, the response time, the hourly rate for out-of-warranty callbacks, and the cost allocation for materials in warranty callbacks are all written. If any of these are blank, you will spend the next three years in disputes.

**Hiring a W2 crew before volume justifies it.** The first in-house crew is usually hired at 10 to 12 installs per month, which is below the breakeven. The crew then sits at 60 percent utilisation for the first six months, loses money, and the owner concludes that in-house is a bad model. Volume first, then crew.

**Treating subcontractors as fungible.** The industry has subcontractors across a wide quality band. The best run their own continuity, show up when they say they will, communicate proactively, and warranty their work. The worst double-book, skip warranty callbacks, and damage roofs. Price is not a reliable quality indicator. Vet two or three before relying on one; retain the relationship with the ones who perform.

**Not building a transition plan.** If you decide to move from subcontract to in-house, do it with 60 to 90 days of overlap. Hire the crew while the subs are still working, run paired installs, bring the subs down to a defined handoff point. Owners who drop subs on Friday and expect in-house to pick up on Monday almost always see install quality drop and warranty rate spike for the first two quarters.

## What this looks like for a typical US residential solar company

**Early-stage, 8 installs per month, variable:** Subcontract everything. One or two trusted subs, both with warranty-included terms. Total install cost roughly $5,500 per install (8kW averaged, mix of complexity). Monthly install spend: $44,000. Equivalent in-house cost at 8 installs would be $14,500 to $21,000 fully loaded, giving a per-install cost of $1,800 to $2,600 but burning that fixed cost even in a month that only produces 5 installs. Verdict: subcontract.

**Growing, 15 installs per month, steady, concentrated geography:** Transition period. One in-house crew plus one sub for overflow. In-house handles 10 to 12 installs a month, sub handles 3 to 5. In-house cost: $16,500 per month. Sub cost: $20,000 to $25,000 per month. Total install cost: $36,500 to $41,500, or roughly $2,500 to $2,800 per install averaged. Verdict: moving to hybrid, in-house taking the majority.

**Established, 25 installs per month, steady:** Two in-house crews plus sub overflow for complex jobs. In-house cost: $33,000 per month for two crews. Sub cost for 3 to 5 complex installs: $20,000 to $35,000. Total: $53,000 to $68,000 per month, or roughly $2,200 to $2,700 per install averaged. In-house crews run at 90 percent utilisation. Verdict: in-house dominant.

## FAQ

**What is the breakeven volume for the first in-house crew?**
In most US states, 12 to 15 installs per month on steady volume with concentrated geography. California with higher wages and workers comp pushes closer to 15 to 18. Southeastern states with lower wages can hit breakeven at 10 to 12. Run the maths on your actual numbers before committing.

**Should I 1099 my install crew?**
Depends on state. California (via AB5), Washington (ABC test), New Jersey, Massachusetts and a growing list have made 1099 solar installers extremely risky. In those states, the misclassification penalty for a single crew of three can run $50,000 to $150,000 if discovered on audit. In states with looser rules, 1099 can work if the installer is genuinely independent. Talk to a solar-savvy employment lawyer before deciding.

**What hourly rate should a solar foreman and installer earn?**
Current US ranges: foreman $28 to $42 per hour, installer $18 to $28 per hour. Add 22 to 32 percent for taxes and benefits burden. Below the low end, you lose people quickly. Above the high end, you need to justify it with pricing or install efficiency.

**Can I use the same subcontractor for all my installs?**
Yes, but check the state classification rules carefully. If a subcontractor derives more than 50 percent of their revenue from your company, several states' ABC tests will treat them as a de facto employee for classification purposes. Spread your subcontract across two or three to stay compliant and to protect against subcontractor availability risk.

**How do I handle warranty callbacks when I transition from subcontract to in-house?**
Keep the subcontractor on retainer for warranty callbacks on their past installs for the length of their warranty obligation. New in-house installs get in-house warranty response. Trying to absorb the subcontractor's past warranty obligations into your new in-house crew without compensation is a common mistake that burns relationships and legal clarity.
