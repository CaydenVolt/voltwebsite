```
Solar sales rep ramp time: what to expect in the first 90 days
```

## Body

The number owners carry about new solar reps is wrong in two directions. Half expect first-month productivity and fire the rep in week 10 because the numbers aren't there. The other half pay full base for six months while the rep closes nothing and tell themselves it is still ramping. Both outcomes come from not knowing what good actually looks like at each week.

A productive solar sales rep in the US residential market reaches first sale by week 4 to 6, hits 50 percent of a seasoned rep's close volume by week 8 to 10, and reaches 80 to 100 percent of seasoned-rep volume by the end of month 3 to 4. Reps who have not closed a deal by week 8 are usually on a different trajectory, and the decision about whether to keep investing should happen at week 10, not week 24. The reason the ramp is so predictable is that most of the first-90-day failure is in the training and system, not the person.

This piece lays out the week-by-week expectation for a solar sales ramp, the metrics that predict success before the first sale lands, and the three places most ramps break.

## What does the first 90 days actually look like?

The sequence that holds up across most well-run US residential solar teams:

**Weeks 1 to 2: product and pitch training.** The rep learns the pitch, learns the finance product, learns the install process end to end, and sits in on five to ten actual sales calls with senior reps. No independent selling in this period. Shadowing only. Common mistake: shortening this to three or four days because the rep "seems smart." The three-day-trained rep closes 40 to 60 percent of what a two-week-trained rep closes, forever.

**Weeks 3 to 4: paired selling.** The rep runs their own sales calls with a senior rep in the room, either in person or on screen share. The senior rep observes and does not interrupt unless something has gone badly wrong. After each call, 15 minutes of structured feedback. Target: six to ten calls run in this window, with one or two closing. First commission earned.

**Weeks 5 to 8: solo selling with structured review.** The rep runs calls independently. Pipeline reviewed daily for the first week, then every other day. Three times a week the rep listens back to one of their own recorded calls with a sales manager for coaching. Target: 15 to 25 calls run, 2 to 5 deals closed, pipeline being built out to 20 to 30 active prospects.

**Weeks 9 to 12: full desk, light coaching.** The rep is running their own book. Pipeline review once a week, call-recording review weekly. Target: 20 to 35 calls, 4 to 8 deals closed. If the rep is tracking at 50 to 100 percent of seasoned-rep volume by now, they are on the path.

A rep who completes this ramp at the right numbers is going to be a $200,000 to $400,000 per year commission earner in year two if your average system size and your pricing hold up.

## What are the early signals that predict success?

The first sale is not the first signal. Several leading indicators predict ramp outcome one to three weeks before a sale lands.

**Calls per day.** By end of week 4, a ramping rep should be running 2 to 4 full appointments per day consistently. Below that, the appointment flow is broken or the rep is not holding appointments. Above that early is a sign of appointment quality dropping, not strength.

**Appointment-to-sat rate.** The industry median for warm appointments is 60 to 75 percent sat. A new rep should be at or near this rate by week 4. A new rep sitting at 40 percent is likely to be confirming appointments poorly or scheduling them wrong. Fix the confirmation process, not the rep.

**Call duration.** A full solar sales call runs 60 to 90 minutes in person, 45 to 70 minutes online. A new rep whose calls are running 25 to 35 minutes is cutting the call short, usually because they are uncomfortable with the finance section or because they are not asking qualifying questions properly. Pull three recordings and listen.

**Second-call booking rate.** When a homeowner needs time to decide, the strong rep books the second call before leaving the first. By week 6, a ramping rep should be booking 50 to 70 percent of their non-close first calls into a scheduled follow-up. A rep below 30 percent is leaving money on the table and will cap out at low volume.

**Pipeline depth.** By end of week 6, the rep should have 15 to 25 active prospects in the pipeline across different stages. If the pipeline is below 10, the rep is working too narrowly or not following up at all. Early-stage pipeline depth predicts month-three revenue better than first-month closes.

These five indicators run two to three weeks ahead of actual close performance. A manager who watches them can tell by week 6 which reps are going to hit the target and which are not.

## What are the three places ramps break?

Across most new-rep failures, the root cause is in one of three places:

**The finance section of the pitch.** Solar sales rise or fall on the finance explanation. Most reps who fail at ramp fail here, specifically at the moment they move from showing a monthly payment to explaining ownership, tax credit, dealer fees and net cost. If the rep is uncomfortable with the numbers, the homeowner reads the discomfort as hiding something. Fix: 20 hours of pure finance explanation practice in weeks 1 to 2. Role-play the finance section until it is boring. Then role-play it with difficult-homeowner objections for another 10 hours.

**Objection handling at the price reveal.** The second common failure point. The rep walks the homeowner through a strong pitch, lands at the price, and does not have language for the first objection. Common objections to prepare specific scripted responses for: "we need to think about it," "we need to talk to our neighbour who went solar," "it is more than we expected," "we are waiting for prices to come down," "we want to get other quotes." Each of these deserves 30 minutes of practice in week 3 or 4. A rep who fumbles any of these at week 10 is going to continue fumbling them.

**Follow-up discipline.** The silent killer. Many reps close well in the first call but have no system for the 40 to 60 percent of homeowners who need a second conversation. The rep walks out of the first call with the homeowner's attention, then does not follow up for three days, then sends one text, then stops. The homeowner cools, buys elsewhere or does nothing. Fix: a defined 7-day follow-up cadence after a non-close first call. The rep should know on Monday what Tuesday's follow-ups are. The CRM should fire the reminders. The sales manager should audit the follow-up queue weekly.

Fix these three, and the ramp curves straighten.

## What should you pay a solar rep during ramp?

Three philosophies here, each with legitimate uses.

**Full commission, no base.** The traditional model. Rep eats what they kill. In the first 90 days, the rep earns 1 to 3 closes a month at $800 to $1,500 commission each, which gives them $800 to $4,500 of month-one income. For many reps this is not a liveable number. You lose the slow starters, including the slow starters who become top producers in month 6. If you only hire reps who can survive the ramp on commission alone, your talent pool is small.

**Draw against commission.** The compromise. Pay the rep a monthly draw of $3,000 to $5,000 in month 1 and 2, which gets reduced as commission payouts begin. The draw is reconciled against earned commission at the end of each quarter. Rep stays whole during the ramp, you stay honest about the economics. Works well: industry median for the first 90 days. Watch for: reps who have been "on draw" for six months are not making it, and the quarterly reconciliation is going to be painful.

**Full base ramping to commission.** Pay $5,000 to $7,500 per month base for the first 60 days, dropping to commission-only in month 3. Used mostly by premium brand companies hiring reps with strong track records who need runway. Works well when: you are competing with better-paying industries for top sales talent. Does not work for: reps you are hiring on potential alone.

The right structure depends on your sales culture and your talent pool. The wrong structure is the one where you lie to yourself about what the rep is going to earn in month 2 so you can convince them to join. Pay honestly and openly or they will quit when the reality lands.

## What should you measure every week during ramp?

Four metrics per rep, same four every week:

**Appointments run.** The input. Not booked, not scheduled, not pending; actually run. A rep running 10 appointments a week in month 1 is on track. Running 5 is not, and the question is whether the appointment flow is broken, the rep is cancelling appointments or they are not holding themselves accountable to the calendar.

**Sat rate.** Appointments run divided by appointments scheduled. Industry median 60 to 75 percent on warm. If the rep's sat rate is below 50 percent, the issue is upstream: confirmation process, text cadence or appointment quality from the setter.

**Close rate.** Deals closed divided by appointments sat. Industry median for a well-trained rep on warm leads is 25 to 40 percent, hitting that by week 10 to 12. New rep in week 4 is usually 10 to 20 percent, which is normal. If it is still below 15 percent at week 12, the rep is unlikely to make it without specific coaching intervention.

**Pipeline health.** Active prospects by stage. First-call-sat but no close yet: should be 60 to 70 percent of active pipeline. Second-call or deeper: 20 to 30 percent. The ratio tells you whether the rep is advancing deals or just adding first calls that then stall.

Review these four with the rep weekly. The review should take 20 minutes. If it takes an hour, you are coaching the person, not the metric. Do the person-coaching in a separate meeting.

## When should you fire a ramping rep?

Three signals at week 10 to 12 that mean the ramp is not going to make it:

**Appointments run is below 15 per month and the rep is not asking for coaching on that.** The rep is not putting in the work. Nothing you do about pitch or finance will fix the input problem.

**Close rate below 15 percent after 20 or more appointments.** The pitch is broken and the rep has not adjusted after four weeks of coaching. At this point they are unlikely to adjust in weeks 13 to 20 either.

**No pipeline advancement.** The rep is adding first calls but no deal is moving to second call or deeper. This is almost always a follow-up discipline problem. If three weeks of structured follow-up coaching has not fixed it, it will not fix in week 15.

If one of these three is true at week 10, have the honest conversation. If two or three are true, the decision has already been made. Keeping a non-ramping rep on the team past week 12 costs more than firing them because it also damages the rhythm and morale of the reps who are ramping correctly.

The companies that get this right do not let the firing conversation become about surprise. The week-10 review is already on the calendar from day one. The metrics are already being measured weekly. The rep knows the numbers. If the numbers are not there, the conversation is calm because everyone has been looking at the same scoreboard.

## What this looks like for a typical US residential solar company

A rep hired in week 1 at $4,000 per month draw against commission. Weeks 1 to 2 shadowing, weeks 3 to 4 paired. First sale lands end of week 5. By week 8 the rep is closing 3 to 5 deals per month at $1,200 commission each. By week 12 the rep is closing 6 to 9 deals per month and the draw is phased out at the end of the quarter. Total company cost in first 90 days: roughly $12,000 of draw plus $5,000 of training time plus $8,000 to $15,000 of commission paid, offset by $90,000 to $160,000 of installed revenue. Net gross margin contribution in first 90 days: $25,000 to $45,000 against a cash cost of about $25,000. The rep is contribution-positive by month 4.

By month 6 the rep is running 25 to 35 appointments a month, closing 10 to 14 deals and earning $12,000 to $20,000 per month. The rep is paying their own cost 3 to 4 times over at this point. Month 9 the rep is at seasoned productivity.

The reps who do not survive the first 90 days cost the company a defined, bounded amount. The reps who do pay for themselves and the five that didn't.

## FAQ

**Should I hire reps with solar experience or train from scratch?**
Both work. Solar-experienced reps ramp 25 to 40 percent faster but command higher commission rates and come with ingrained pitch habits from their previous company (which may conflict with yours). Reps new to solar take longer to ramp but learn your pitch cleanly. If your training programme is strong, train from scratch. If your training is thin, hire experience.

**What is the right close rate target by month 3?**
25 to 35 percent on warm, well-qualified appointments. 15 to 25 percent on colder appointments. Below those ranges in month 3, the issue is almost always the pitch or the finance section, not the rep.

**How do I protect against a top-performer rep leaving and taking the pipeline?**
Non-solicitation agreements that are enforceable in your state (check with employment counsel; some states, including California, void most of these). More importantly, build the pipeline in the CRM, not in the rep's personal relationships. If the rep's whole pipeline is in their phone and their head, you have already lost it when they leave. Enforce weekly CRM updates with teeth.

**Should sales reps ride along to installs?**
Yes, in weeks 1 to 2 of ramp. One or two install days gives the rep real understanding of the product they are selling and the install process they are promising. After ramp, no; the rep's time is better spent in front of homeowners.

**What should I pay for a top-performer solar rep?**
Current US ranges: a seasoned rep in a mid-market closes 10 to 15 deals a month at 4 to 7 percent of system revenue in commission. On an average $25,000 system, that is $1,000 to $1,750 per close, or $120,000 to $300,000 a year. Top producers at premium brands exceed this. If you are offering below the floor, your rep is already looking.
