The audit finding we see most often on solar Google Ads accounts is the same two words: homepage destination. The paid search campaign is pointing at the company's homepage, the homepage was built for everyone, and the ad click, which specifically asked for "solar installer in [town]", lands on a page that talks to no one in particular.

The result is a two to four percent form conversion rate on paid traffic, when it should be six to ten percent. On a $200 cost per raw lead that is the difference between $200 leads and $100 leads. On $5,000 a month of ad spend, that is 25 extra leads a month, every month, for the cost of a few landing pages.

This piece lays out why the mismatch happens, what a matched landing page actually carries, and three solar landing pages taken apart.

## Why does the homepage underperform on paid traffic?

The homepage has a different job. It needs to serve three audiences in roughly equal weight: the organic visitor who typed your company name, the referral visitor who was handed your card, and the researcher who has not decided to buy anything from anyone. The homepage is a honest introduction to the company, which is the right thing for those three audiences.

A paid search click is none of those. It is a visitor who typed "solar quote near me" or "cost of solar panels for my house" into Google at 10 pm on a Tuesday. They have intent, they have a question, and they clicked an ad because it promised to answer the question. If the page they land on instead introduces the company and asks them to scroll through four services and read about the owner's accreditations, the window closes. The visitor is one tab away from a competitor's page that answers the question in the first screen.

The gap between "introduce me to a company" and "answer my question and show me the next step" is the whole landing page problem.

## What does a matched landing page actually carry?

Four things, in roughly this order from top of page to bottom:

**The ad's exact headline.** If the ad said "Residential Solar in Austin from $89/month", the page's H1 should say "Residential Solar in Austin from $89/month" or a near-identical variation. The visitor's brain is scanning for continuity. If the headline on the page says "Welcome to Acme Solar" instead, the visitor has to translate, and the translation is a one-second window where they decide if they are in the right place.

**One form.** Not three. Not a sidebar form plus a hero form plus a footer form. One form, the one that collects just enough information for your rep to call back: address, name, phone, email. Four fields, maybe five if you need utility bill or roof type for routing. Every extra field drops conversion rate by roughly 2 to 5 percent, so a seven-field form converts at perhaps 60 percent of a four-field form on the same traffic.

**Proof above the fold.** The visitor scrolled to the price, saw the form, and now needs a reason to trust that filling it in is worth their phone number. Proof that works, in order of weight:

- Named local projects. "Over 80 installs in Pflugerville and Round Rock" beats "100+ installs in Texas" by a margin, because the first names a town the visitor recognises.
- Review count and star rating with the Google logo. 4.9/5 across 60+ Google reviews is a stronger proof than a logo wall.
- Badges that mean something: NABCEP accreditation, state licence number, insurance details. Not generic trust seals.
- One short specific testimonial, preferably with the customer's first name and town. Not a glowing paragraph; one sentence that mentions a specific outcome.

**A single call to action.** The whole page's job is to drive the one action: submit the form. Not "learn more", not "download our guide", not "take our solar calculator quiz". One form, one button, repeated at natural breakpoints down the page.

What a matched landing page should not carry:

- Site-wide navigation that lets the visitor click away to the About page, the Services page, the Blog, the gallery. If they click, they are gone.
- A hero image of panels on a roof unrelated to the visitor's town or house. Stock photos of solar panels no longer carry weight.
- A general "contact us" option that competes with the main form. Pick one path and defend it.
- Three different lead magnets. Each one is a choice; choice is friction; friction is dropoff.
- A long form the visitor fills before they understand what they are getting.

A homepage carries all of the above because the homepage serves many audiences. A landing page sheds all of it because it serves one.

## Three solar landing pages taken apart

**The $89/month residential page.** This is the archetype that works for a company running a lease or long-term loan product. The ad promises a monthly payment, the H1 repeats it, the form sits in the top-right of the hero, and the proof is a line directly under the headline that reads something like "Join 240 Austin homeowners already saving on their bill". No navigation. Below the fold, three short sections: "How it works" in three steps, "What our customers say" with two sentence-length quotes, "Frequently asked" with four questions that would otherwise be form-abandonment reasons. The second CTA repeats at the bottom. The page's whole job is to turn an interested visitor into a form fill in under 90 seconds.

Where it goes wrong when operators build their own: they add a fourth proof section, a chat widget, a "see our installations" gallery link in the hero, and the form grows a utility-provider dropdown with 60 options.

**The battery-only page.** Battery storage is a different sale. The ad promises resilience or backup during outages, which is a different motivation from monthly savings. The hero carries the exact ad promise, the form asks for home address and current solar status (because a visitor who already has solar is a strong battery lead), and the proof is grid-outage-focused: "40+ battery installs in Harris County survived Hurricane Beryl" or similar. Below the fold, a short diagram showing how the battery sits in the home electrical system, a two-sentence FAQ about the time to install, and the second CTA.

Where it goes wrong: the page tries to also sell solar. A battery visitor who already has solar closes the tab when the first scroll shows solar panels.

**The EV charger page.** The cheapest lead-cost product and often the one where the matched landing page matters least, because the visitor's intent is narrow and the sale is simple. The hero carries the ad promise ("EV charger installed from $699"), the form asks for home address and vehicle make, the proof is a quick-install promise ("most installs completed in a single afternoon"), and the second CTA sits right below the proof. The page is often half the length of the residential solar page.

Where it goes wrong: operators try to upsell solar from the EV charger page. The EV charger visitor is not thinking about panels yet. Save the solar conversation for the follow-up email, not the first page.

## What is the right conversion rate benchmark?

For a matched landing page carrying everything above, these ranges hold up in audited solar accounts:

- Residential install: 6 to 10 percent form conversion on paid traffic
- Battery storage: 5 to 9 percent
- EV charger: 8 to 14 percent (narrower intent, simpler sale)
- Brand traffic: 15 to 30 percent (visitor already knows the company)

If your rate is well below these ranges, the landing page is the issue. If it is at the low end of the range, the ad copy or targeting is partly to blame. If it is well above, verify your tracking is counting the right event; sometimes the number is inflated by double-counting.

## How many landing pages should a solar account have?

The pattern that works for most US residential installers:

- One landing page per product (residential, battery, EV charger). Three pages.
- One per core service area or metro region. Not one per town. One per metro.
- One brand-defence page for the "your company name" campaign.

For a company covering one metro and three products, that is four pages total. For a company covering three metros and three products, nine pages. More than that and the maintenance becomes its own job.

Templating the pages so the hero text and local proof are variables rather than hard-coded content keeps the maintenance cost down. The structure stays the same; the town names, project counts and local proof change.

## FAQ

**Can I use my service page as the landing page?**
It depends on what your service page actually is. If it has the H1, the form, the proof and the single CTA, yes. Most service pages do not; they live inside the main site navigation and were built to inform rather than convert. Audit the specific page against the four requirements above. If it fails any of them, build a dedicated landing page instead.

**Should the landing page have a header and footer?**
A minimal header with the logo (not clickable) and a phone number, yes. A full site footer, no. The footer is where the visitor's eye goes when they have decided not to convert, and a footer with sitemap links hands them an exit. A thin footer with the privacy policy, terms, and nothing else keeps the exit paths closed.

**How long should the landing page be?**
Long enough to earn trust, short enough to keep the visitor's attention. For residential solar, 800 to 1,500 words plus visuals is a working range. Battery and EV pages tend to run shorter. The page should load in under two seconds on mobile; if a visual budget is pushing load time past that, something is being overdone.

**Should I run a different landing page for mobile?**
Not a separate page, a responsive one that was designed mobile-first. 60 to 75 percent of solar Google Ads traffic in the US is on mobile. If the form sits below three screens of hero image on a phone, the form may as well not exist.

**Do I need a video on the landing page?**
Only if the video is doing one of two jobs: showing a real customer in a named town saying one specific thing, or showing a 30-second explainer of how the install happens. A hero video of panels being installed with background music is decorative and often slows the page without lifting conversion. If you cannot name what the video does for conversion, it is costing you load time and attention.
