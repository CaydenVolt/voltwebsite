Google Ads is the fastest way for a solar contractor to generate enquiries, and the fastest way to waste money. A single solar close is worth tens of thousands, which means a well-run competitor can rationally bid a great deal, and the auction settles where only good operators profit. The difference between a profitable account and an expensive education is the build. Not the budget.

This is the page we would hand to a new ads manager taking over a solar account on their first day.

## Before you spend a dollar

Three things decide whether paid traffic converts, and none of them live in the ad account. Skipping them is how most contractors conclude that Google Ads does not work for solar.

1. **Response time under five minutes.** You are paying up to $300 for an enquiry. Answering it tomorrow is burning it. The full argument is in [speed to lead for solar companies](/blog/speed-to-lead-for-solar-companies).
2. **A review count that stands comparison.** Paid visitors compare you against the map pack results on the same screen. Arriving with eleven reviews against competitors with two hundred means paying for clicks that were never going to convert.
3. **A landing page that matches the ad.** Not the homepage. One form, one offer, proof above the fold, and the headline of the ad repeated at the top of the page.

Every one of those three raises the return on the same ad spend. Fixing them first is not a delay. It is the difference between an account that pays for itself and one that funds an education.

## How should the account actually be structured?

Shallow and specific. Solar accounts fail from over-complication far more often than from under-optimisation.

| Campaign | Ad groups | Sends to |
|---|---|---|
| Search: residential install | solar installers, solar panel installation, solar company | /solar-installation landing page |
| Search: battery | home battery, battery storage, solar battery | /battery-storage landing page |
| Search: EV charger | ev charger installation, home charger | /ev-charger landing page |
| Search: brand | your company name and misspellings | Homepage |

Four campaigns, each with its own budget. Shared budgets starve the campaigns that convert and feed the ones that don't.

Each campaign holds two to five ad groups. Each ad group holds one theme and three to five keywords in that theme. If you cannot write one ad that suits every keyword in an ad group, it is two ad groups.

The brand campaign always runs. Competitors bid on your name; without a brand campaign you pay for the click anyway, in lost traffic.

## Which keywords actually generate installs?

High-intent: terms with a buying verb or a local qualifier. "install solar panels", "solar installer near me", "solar quote [city]", "cost of solar panels for home". These convert.

Low-intent: terms a reader is using to learn, not to buy. "how do solar panels work", "diy solar", "solar panels for sale". These do not convert for a contractor and should either sit outside the keyword list or be bid at a tenth of the install-intent keywords.

Match types: start with exact and phrase match. Broad match hands Google permission to spend your budget on close-but-wrong searches, and in solar the close-but-wrong searches are expensive. Move to broad match only once you have at least a month of conversion data and a mature negative list.

## The negative keyword list every solar account starts with

Six categories almost every solar account should exclude at the account level before the first click:

- **DIY and research.** diy, how to, how do, build your own, kit, kits
- **Jobs and education.** jobs, careers, salary, apprenticeship, course, courses, training, certification
- **Free and cheap.** free, cheap, bargain, lowest price, discount, used, second hand
- **Downloadable and digital.** download, pdf, software, calculator (unless you own the calculator)
- **Research and comparison intent that will not convert.** reddit, forum, youtube, wikipedia
- **Unrelated solar terms.** solar eclipse, solar system (astronomy), solar lights (garden)

Then mine the search terms report weekly for the first three months. Every search term that triggered an ad and did not convert, that you can see was never going to, becomes a new negative. The list grows into a filter that spends the budget only on traffic worth spending it on.

## How do you know if it is working?

Not cost per click. Not even cost per lead. Cost per sat appointment.

The right tracking stack:

- **GA4** with enhanced conversions on, so first-party data closes the loop when cookies are blocked.
- **Call tracking** on the number in the ads. Dynamic number insertion if you are serious; a dedicated number per campaign at minimum.
- **Offline conversion imports** from your CRM, so Google learns which clicks became sat appointments and closed deals, not just which clicks became form fills.

Most accounts optimise on form submission and ignore the step after. The problem is that half of form submissions never pick up the phone, and the half that do include tyre-kickers that will never book a survey. Importing the sat-appointment event back into Google is what tells its bidding model which clicks are actually worth something. Without it, Google is optimising for the wrong outcome. ## What should the budget look like?

The industry floor for a single-product, single-metro solar campaign is around $1,500 a month in ad spend. Below that, Google's bidding does not have enough signal to optimise and the account looks worse than it is. Above about $5,000 a month in a single metro, you are usually running into competitor floors and the next dollar earns less.

Start narrow. One campaign, one metro, one landing page, 30 days. Judge on cost per sat appointment, not on activity. Scale only when the single campaign is paying for itself.

## The bid-strategy progression

Four stages, in order. Skipping stages is how accounts stall.

1. **Manual CPC for the first 30 days.** You need the control to see what is happening at the keyword level. Automated strategies hide the detail that matters when the account is new.
2. **Maximise clicks for a short window** only if conversion volume is too thin for the automated strategies to learn, and only on campaigns with clean negatives.
3. **Maximise conversions** once you have 15+ conversions in a rolling 30 days per campaign.
4. **Target CPA or target ROAS** once you have 30+ conversions and offline imports are flowing, so Google can bid against the sat-appointment event instead of the form fill.

Jumping to target CPA on week two is the single most common reason a new solar account burns $5,000 learning nothing.

## FAQ

**Do I need a dedicated landing page, or will the homepage do?**
A dedicated page converts two to four times better than the homepage for most solar contractors. The homepage carries five messages competing for attention; a landing page carries one. If budget is tight, start with the homepage and ship a landing page as the first upgrade.

**How much should a solar lead cost on Google Ads?**
Raw leads commonly run $80 to $300 depending on the metro and the product. Residential installs are the most expensive; battery-only and EV charger leads are cheaper. The number that matters, though, is cost per sat appointment, which is usually two to four times the raw lead cost once the half of leads that never book are filtered out.

**Is Performance Max worth running for a solar contractor?**
Not for an account that is still proving search works. Performance Max without strong conversion data spends into whichever placement looks cheapest, which for solar is usually brand traffic you already own. Prove search, then add Performance Max as a controlled extension once you have 30+ monthly conversions.

**Can one person manage this in-house?**
Yes, if they can commit four to six hours a week to it and they have the account access to make changes without approvals. If they have other jobs and get to the account monthly, the account will quietly drift and the budget will quietly waste. The honest cost of in-house is a part-time salary; the honest cost of agency management is $1,000 to $3,000 a month plus the ad spend. Both work. The middle option, where someone is "going to look at it when they get a chance", does not.
